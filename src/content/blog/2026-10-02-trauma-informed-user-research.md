## When does user research need a trauma-informed approach?

Trauma-informed user research helps you plan sensitive work without asking participants to carry unnecessary risk. It designs for safety, trust, choice, collaboration, empowerment and cultural consideration; it does not diagnose or treat trauma. The [Department of Health and Social Care’s definition](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/working-definition-of-trauma-informed-practice/working-definition-of-trauma-informed-practice) sets out these six principles.

A sensitive subject alone does not determine the approach. Assess foreseeable distress, stigma, power imbalance, the consequences of disclosure, participant circumstances, method, setting and data sensitivity. Use enhanced planning where someone could reasonably become distressed, disclose harm or lose control of sensitive information.

This work sits alongside established [UX research methods](/blog/ux-research-methods/). Safeguarding, legal and clinical duties vary by organisation and jurisdiction, so involve the appropriate safeguarding lead, legal adviser or subject specialist. This is practical guidance for ethical UX research, not legal or clinical advice.

## Use the safeguarding canvas before you recruit

Complete this Sensitive-Research Safeguarding Canvas before recruitment. Rate each area low, medium or high. For every medium or high rating, record a control, named owner and decision deadline. The Department for Education recommends documenting safeguarding risks in a risk register and distinguishes harm caused during research, harm disclosed during research and escalation concerns in its [participant safeguarding guidance](https://user-research.education.gov.uk/guidance/ethics-and-safeguarding/participant-safeguarding).

The canvas is a planning record, not ethics approval or a substitute for organisational safeguarding processes.

## Build safety and choice into recruitment and consent

Recruitment should state the broad topic, activities, time commitment, incentive and available choices. Do not require people to disclose trauma or difficult experiences to qualify. Clear information lets people decide whether the research is right for them.

Give participants accessible, plain-language information before they agree. Cover the research purpose, what will happen, recordings, observers, data use, sharing, retention, voluntary participation, withdrawal and limits to confidentiality. [GOV.UK’s consent guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/getting-users-consent-for-research) includes voluntary participation and the ability to stop or withdraw as part of informed consent.

Offer meaningful control where feasible:

- Mode, timing and accessibility adjustments
- Camera on or off
- Recording permission separate from participation
- Breaks, skipped questions and stopping without penalty
- No requirement to explain why they are stopping

Collect only the screening and contact data needed for the study. Agree storage, access, retention and deletion through your organisation’s data-protection process. See [UK GDPR compliance for user research participants](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/uk-gdpr-compliance-user-research-participants/) for related considerations.

Community partners may improve trust and reach, but should not pressure people to participate. Incentives should recognise time and contribution without making withdrawal feel costly; see [user research participant incentives](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/user-research-participant-incentives-uk/).

## Moderate sensitive sessions without becoming a therapist

A moderator’s role is to understand a service experience, not provide counselling, investigate a disclosure or promise confidentiality beyond the consent terms. Set that boundary before fieldwork and repeat it when needed.

Order a discussion guide from less sensitive to more sensitive material. Ask permission before a transition: “The next questions are about contacting support. Would you like to continue, skip this section or take a break?” Focus on the service rather than inviting unnecessary personal detail.

GOV.UK advises researchers to remind participants regularly that they can stop at any time in emotionally sensitive sessions. Its [guidance on sensitive subjects](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/researching-emotionally-sensitive-subjects) also addresses breaks, support signposting and researcher wellbeing. The Australian Institute of Family Studies recommends having a plan if participants become distressed and giving them control over data collection in its [trauma-informed research guide](https://aifs.gov.au/resources/practice-guides/how-do-trauma-informed-research-and-evaluation).

Use this copyable protocol, adapted to your organisation’s escalation process.

> **Stop / pause / escalate protocol**
>
> 1. Notice: “I can see this may be difficult. We can pause.”
> 2. Offer choices: “Would you prefer to skip this, take a break, stop now or continue?”
> 3. Do not probe for detail or attempt therapy.
> 4. If continuing, return to a less sensitive task only with the participant’s agreement.
> 5. If stopping, explain how notes and recordings will be handled, then share agreed support information.
> 6. If there is a safeguarding concern, follow the named escalation route and immediate-danger procedure.

End the session if the participant asks to stop, cannot give informed agreement to continue, or your protocol requires escalation. Make conditions safer with a private, accessible setting, limited observers, explicit recording consent and time to close calmly.

Method choice also matters when researching sensitive topics. Compare [usability testing vs user interviews](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/usability-testing-vs-user-interviews/) before asking participants to narrate difficult experiences. Where interpreters are involved, plan roles, confidentiality and participant control using guidance on [multilingual UX research interviews through interpreters](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/multilingual-ux-research-interviews-through-interpreters/). Adapt wording with subject-matter, safeguarding and lived-experience expertise; no script is universal.

## Protect participants in analysis, sharing and follow-up

Safeguarding continues after the session. Separate identifying details from research evidence where possible. Do not retain sensitive detail in notes, clips or findings merely because it is memorable.

Apply consent boundaries to observers, recordings, transcription, repositories and stakeholder playback. Review outputs for re-identification risk, especially when a small group or distinctive experience makes anonymity fragile. Describe a service barrier without turning someone’s account into spectacle.

Plan how participants can request withdrawal, who receives the request and how it will be actioned. Confirm whether you will make post-session contact and provide current support-resource signposting. Do not imply that the research team can provide ongoing support unless it has agreed capacity and remit.

Good synthesis focuses on decisions: what participants needed, where the service failed and what should change. See [insight to impact](/blog/insight-to-impact/) for turning evidence into accountable action.

## Support moderators and make the protocol operational

Name responsibilities before fieldwork: research lead, moderator, note-taker, safeguarding escalation contact and data owner. Brief the team on consent wording, session boundaries, distress responses and who can stop a session.

Plan training, session spacing and debriefs. Moderators need a route to opt out when a topic is unsuitable for them. This is a delivery requirement, not a personal failing. Human judgement is especially relevant where safeguarding decisions may arise; consider this when comparing [AI-moderated vs human-moderated research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/ai-moderated-vs-human-moderated-research/).

Before launch, confirm:

- The canvas is complete and controls have owners.
- Consent wording is reviewed and support resources are current.
- The escalation contact and route have been tested.
- The team is briefed and debrief time is booked.
- The data plan is approved.

## Trauma-informed user research checklist

Copy this into your research plan:

- Have we assessed whether enhanced safeguards are needed?
- Have relevant specialists reviewed the plan?
- Does recruitment disclose the broad topic and available choices?
- Does consent cover data, recording, withdrawal and confidentiality limits?
- Can participants pause, skip or stop without penalty?
- Are distress and safeguarding routes named and tested?
- Is sensitive data minimised and access controlled?
- Are moderator boundaries and support resources clear?
- Are researcher debriefs planned?

Use the checklist at the start of [product discovery](/blog/product-discovery/), before recruitment makes changes harder.

### What is trauma-informed user research?

It is an approach that anticipates potential trauma impacts and builds safety, transparency, choice, collaboration and safeguards into research. It is not trauma therapy.

### Do all sensitive user research projects need a trauma-informed approach?

No. Assess foreseeable risk and participant context rather than applying a label mechanically. The safeguarding canvas helps you choose proportionate controls.

### What should a moderator do if a participant becomes distressed?

Pause or stop, offer choices, avoid probing or providing therapy, share pre-agreed support information and follow the organisation’s safeguarding escalation protocol where necessary.

### Can trauma-informed research be conducted remotely?

Yes. Give extra attention to privacy, participant control, recording, access to support and the steps your team will take if a safeguarding concern arises.
