## What continuous discovery interviews are—and are not

Continuous discovery interviews for product teams are recurring customer conversations connected to a current product outcome or decision. Product Talk defines the minimum practice as weekly customer touchpoints by the team building the product ([Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/getting-started-with-discovery/)).

Cadence alone is not enough. Each conversation needs a live learning goal: understand a failed onboarding step, examine an opportunity or test an assumption. This creates a lightweight loop within [continuous product discovery](/blog/product-discovery/): listen, interpret, decide and return to customers.

These interviews are not sales calls, customer-success check-ins, usability tests or formal research studies. Those methods answer different questions. Continuous interviewing keeps a team close to customer behaviour, but it does not establish prevalence or replace methods needed for sensitive, high-stakes or representative questions.

## Choose a cadence the team can sustain

Start with one protected weekly interview slot, a short pre-brief and a synthesis block immediately afterwards. A continuous discovery interview cadence only works when the team can maintain it.

Use this weekly loop:

1. Select a question tied to an outcome.
2. Confirm a suitable participant and backup.
3. Run the interview.
4. Complete an interview snapshot.
5. Update the decision log.
6. Assign the next question and owner.

Treat a missed session as an operational signal. Record whether recruitment, scheduling, consent, ownership, competing priorities or an unclear question caused it. Weekly contact gives continuity, not proof of a broad pattern: one conversation can challenge an assumption, but cannot establish how common an experience is.

An unreliable cadence and findings that do not influence decisions contribute to [research debt in product teams](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/research-debt-in-product-teams/): unanswered questions and unsupported choices that slow later work.

## Build a continuous discovery recruitment pipeline

Work backwards from the decision. Define who can answer the current question, then select a recruitment route. If you are investigating first-week onboarding, recruit people who have recently attempted onboarding rather than whoever is easiest to contact.

Use repeatable sources that fit your product and privacy arrangements:

- An opted-in customer panel
- An in-product invitation
- A support follow-up
- A referral from a customer-facing team

A documented Product Talk example describes Zonar Systems establishing recurring interviews after automating recruitment ([Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/automating-customer-interviews-zonar/)). Automation is not a requirement; the practical lesson is that recurring interviews need a repeatable recruitment process. You can [build and maintain a UX research panel](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/ux-research-panel-management/) alongside other routes rather than relying on a panel alone.

Track these fields separately in a spreadsheet or research-operations tool:

| Pipeline check | Record |
|---|---|
| Eligibility | Segment, relevant experience and screener status |
| Privacy | Consent or other applicable privacy status |
| Invitation | Channel, date, response and scheduling status |
| Attendance | Confirmed, completed, cancelled or no-show |
| Recontact | Cooldown date and contact owner |

Keep suitable confirmed participants and backups available. Rotate segments and sources so enthusiastic regulars or contacts already close to the team do not shape the programme. Give one person ownership of the pipeline and review its health at a fixed weekly checkpoint. This is [research operations](/blog/research-operations/) work: making customer access reliable enough for the team to learn.

If processing relies on consent, the ICO says organisations need an audit trail showing how and when consent was given ([ICO guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/how-should-we-obtain-record-and-manage-consent/)). Check the lawful basis and policy that apply to your activity.

## Share ownership without losing research quality

People making product decisions should hear from customers directly. That does not mean every team member should interview without support.

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Question owner | Defines the decision and learning question |
| Facilitator | Leads the conversation and manages scope |
| Observer or note-taker | Captures evidence without interrupting |
| Recruitment owner | Maintains participant flow and status |
| Decision owner | Records action and review point |

Where research expertise is available, a researcher can set guardrails, coach facilitators and review the practice. Rotate participation while retaining a shared brief, snapshot format and decision log.

Brief observers not to sell, defend a design, resolve support issues or begin an internal debate during the session. [Why customer interviews should not simply be delegated](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/why-you-shouldnt-delegate-customer-interviews/) makes the case for shared accountability, not for excluding research expertise.

## Run each interview around one learning goal

Turn a broad question into a prompt about a specific past event. Rather than asking, “Would you use a progress tracker?”, ask, “Tell me about the last time you tried to work out what to do next.” Product Talk recommends translating assumptions into a story-based interview question ([Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/cdh-book-club-april-2026/)).

Use temporal follow-ups to recover behaviour and context:

- What happened first?
- What did you do next?
- What happened immediately before that?
- What information did you use?
- What made the task easier or harder?

Keep the guide lean: an introduction and purpose, recording permission where applicable, one core story prompt, behavioural probes and a close. Avoid leading questions, feature pitches and hypothetical purchase claims. A feature request can point to a problem, but the surrounding story is needed to assess it.

Record consent and recording status under your policy. Keep identifiers to a minimum in working notes and separate contact details from synthesis material. The ICO advises organisations to identify the minimum personal data needed and use anonymous or pseudonymous information where appropriate ([ICO guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/the-research-provisions/what-are-the-appropriate-safeguards/)). If an interview cannot answer the question well, choose from wider [UX research methods](/blog/ux-research-methods/).

## Turn each conversation into a decision-ready record

Reserve time immediately after the session for collaborative synthesis. Product Talk describes an interview snapshot as a one-page template for quickly capturing what was learned ([Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/interview-snapshot/)).

| Snapshot field | Capture |
|---|---|
| Participant context | Relevant role, segment and situation |
| Observed story | What happened, in sequence |
| Needs or pain points | Needs evidenced by that story |
| Notable evidence | Notes or approved source references |
| Surprises | What challenged an expectation |
| Unanswered questions | What needs investigation |

Separate observations from interpretations. “The participant searched help content before starting” is an observation. “Instructions are unclear” is an interpretation that needs further evidence.

Then add a decision-log row:

| Date | Decision or assumption | Evidence references | Interpretation | Confidence | Owner | Action | Review trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |

Evidence may update an opportunity, prompt another interview, justify an experiment or leave the decision unchanged. The aim is [turning insight into impact](/blog/insight-to-impact/), not producing a new report every week.

## Use the six-week operating worksheet

This filled worksheet is hypothetical. It is a reusable operating model for a fictional B2B onboarding team, not a reported client result.

| Week | Outcome, question and participant | Recruitment and roles | Interview record and next action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Outcome: improve first-workspace setup. What happens when a new administrator first sets up a workspace? | Panel; product manager owns the question, researcher facilitates, designer notes. | Tuesday slot; snapshot `W1-setup`; add setup sequence to decision log; recruit another recent administrator. |
| 2 | What prompts the first setup attempt? Recent administrator. | In-product invitation; designer facilitates, product manager observes. | Tuesday slot; snapshot `W2-trigger`; log the trigger; probe handover context next week. |
| 3 | What information is available at handover? Recent administrator. | Support follow-up; researcher coaches, designer facilitates. | Tuesday slot; snapshot `W3-handover`; add evidence references; recruit from a different organisation size. |
| 4 | Does the story differ by segment? Administrator from the new segment. | Panel; product manager facilitates, researcher notes. | Tuesday slot; snapshot `W4-segment`; compare stories without declaring a pattern; define the next uncertainty. |
| 5 | What happens when permissions are delegated? Administrator involved in delegation. | Customer-team referral; designer facilitates. | Tuesday slot; snapshot `W5-permissions`; update the log; decide whether another interview or an experiment is needed. |
| 6 | Is the operating system working? Team review. | Recruitment owner brings pipeline status; decision owner leads review. | Review missed sessions, coverage, snapshots, decision links and the next six-week queue. |

At the weekly review, check that a suitable session is booked, segment coverage is visible, synthesis is complete, evidence connects to a decision and the next owner is named. Lyssna identifies recruitment and consistency as common barriers to continuous interviewing ([Lyssna](https://www.lyssna.com/blog/continuous-interviewing/)). If the cadence breaks down, address the pipeline or ownership constraint before increasing the target.

## Know when to escalate into formal research

| Situation | Appropriate response |
|---|---|
| Low-risk exploratory question with accessible participants | Continue the weekly loop |
| Team needs interviewing or analysis support | Seek researcher coaching |
| High-stakes decision, contradictory evidence, representative coverage or prevalence needed | Commission a bounded formal study |
| Sensitive topic, distress risk, privacy, ethics or safeguarding concern | Pause for specialist review |

Assess accessibility needs, specialist recruitment and possible participant distress before proceeding. Use [trauma-informed research safeguards](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/trauma-informed-user-research/) where relevant.

Continuous interviews keep teams close to customers; formal research supplies added rigour when the question and consequences demand it. Put one protected interview, synthesis block and decision-log review in next week’s calendar.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should product teams run continuous discovery interviews?

Aim for a weekly customer touchpoint. Prioritise consistency and participant fit over an arbitrary count, and investigate missed sessions.

### Who should conduct continuous discovery interviews?

Decision-makers should participate directly. A trained researcher should set guardrails, coach or lead when the work carries greater risk or complexity.

### How do you recruit customers for interviews every week?

Build a consent-aware pipeline with eligibility criteria, repeatable channels, backups, cooldown tracking and one accountable owner.

### How should teams synthesise weekly customer interviews?

Complete a short snapshot after every session, retain evidence references and update a running decision log.

### When are continuous interviews not enough?

Escalate when the work is sensitive, high stakes or regulated, requires representative coverage, estimates prevalence or exceeds the limits of a lightweight loop.
