## What buyer interviews can—and cannot—validate

B2B market entry validation with buyer interviews helps you make a specific decision: enter a named market or segment, run a limited pilot, research further or pause. It is not a vote on whether people like an idea.

Interviews can reveal problem recognition, current alternatives, purchase triggers, budget routes, decision roles and local barriers. Use them as one part of [B2B market research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/b2b-market-research/), not as standalone proof. The U.S. International Trade Administration describes primary research as direct contact with prospective buyers and representatives, which should be combined with secondary evidence ([International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/conducting-market-research)).

Interviews cannot establish market size, future purchasing behaviour or willingness to pay. A participant may welcome a concept yet lack budget, authority or a reason to change. Pair findings with market data, regulatory checks, partner due diligence and a behavioural test such as a procurement review or paid pilot.

## Define the entry decision and evidence thresholds first

Write the proposed geography, target-account segment, use case, offer and entry route as assumptions. Then state the decision the research must support.

For example, a hypothetical software company might ask: “Can we reach mid-sized manufacturers in Country A with a compliance workflow product through direct sales, and should we fund a six-month pilot?” This creates testable questions:

- Is the problem urgent enough to prompt action?
- Is there current spend or a costly workaround?
- Can the buying committee approve this supplier type?
- Can the offer displace an established alternative?
- What localisation or delivery changes are required?
- Are there external constraints that make entry impractical?

Set minimum evidence and disconfirming conditions before recruitment. Encouraging comments can otherwise be mistaken for proof. GOV.UK recommends defining what you need to know for an informed next decision ([GOV.UK User Research Community](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/plan-round-of-user-research)).

The thresholds in the tables below are illustrative for the hypothetical company, not universal targets. Set your own around sales capacity, delivery model, risk tolerance and investment case. [Product research methods](/blog/product-research/) can help distinguish an unanswered question from one that does not affect the decision.

## Recruit the buying system, not just enthusiastic users

A user may understand the problem but not know whether the organisation can approve a supplier. Recruit across the buying system: people who experience the problem, shape the shortlist, control budget and introduce friction.

Build a recruitment matrix across account size, subsegment, region and role. Include problem owners, frequent users, budget holders and relevant procurement, security, legal or finance stakeholders. Where resellers, distributors or implementation partners affect access, include them too.

Screen for recent behaviour. Ask whether a participant has evaluated, renewed, purchased or implemented the category within a defined period. A job title alone is weak evidence of involvement.

Record every recruitment route. Warm-network and partner referrals can be useful, but analyse them separately from independent recruits so that referral bias remains visible. There is no universal interview count: recruit in purposeful waves, review role and segment coverage, and continue where material gaps remain. [Customer segmentation research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-to-run-a-customer-segmentation-research-study/) can help identify account differences that matter.

## Ask market-entry questions about real buying behaviour

Start with the participant’s role and their last relevant purchase or evaluation. Do not introduce the proposed offer until you understand the existing situation. GOV.UK recommends stories and real examples over generalities in in-depth interviews ([GOV.UK User Research Community](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews)).

Use questions such as:

- “Tell me about the last time this problem caused a delay, cost or risk.”
- “What triggered the evaluation, and what happened if nothing changed?”
- “How was the issue managed before?”
- “Which suppliers or internal alternatives reached the shortlist?”
- “Who initiated, influenced, blocked, funded and signed off the decision?”
- “What evidence was needed for approval?”
- “What contracting, integration, security, language or support requirements affected the choice?”
- “What would need to happen for a new supplier to receive a serious next step?”

Probe for dates, sequence, documents and consequences. “We need better reporting” is an opinion. “We changed process after an audit finding, then procurement required a security review” is reported behaviour with follow-up points to verify.

Once the context is clear, show a neutral concept. Ask where it fits, what proof is missing, which objections it creates and what a realistic next commitment would be. [B2B customer journey mapping research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/b2b-customer-journey-mapping-research/) helps make that sequence visible across roles.

## Run international interviews without losing local meaning

Use the participant’s preferred language where feasible. Brief interpreters to translate fully and neutrally, rather than summarising objections or explaining the offer.

Pilot the guide locally. Category terms and examples may not travel cleanly. A pilot can expose unclear terminology, assumptions that conflict with the local buying process and questions that invite polite agreement. Ask about specific decisions and events rather than attributing behaviour to national culture.

For each observation, record whether it is verbatim, translated or interpreter-mediated. Obtain informed consent and define recording, storage, access and anonymisation arrangements appropriate to the jurisdictions involved.

Treat legal, tax, regulatory and data-processing statements as leads for qualified verification. The International Trade Administration notes that regulatory, logistical and cultural factors can affect market entry ([International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/selecting-international-markets)). Interview evidence does not replace country, company or transaction due diligence ([International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/perform-due-diligence)). For moderation choices, see [interviews through interpreters](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/multilingual-ux-research-interviews-through-interpreters/).

## Turn interviews into an evidence ledger

An evidence ledger connects each observation to a decision condition. It prevents a memorable quote from carrying more weight than it deserves.

Tag observations by hypothesis, buyer role, segment, market, evidence type and confidence. Keep recalled behaviour, documented process, opinion, concept reaction and researcher inference separate. Record counter-evidence plainly.

This completed hypothetical ledger is reusable: replace the entries with your evidence after each interview wave.

| Hypothesis | Role and evidence | Evidence type | Counter-evidence or gap | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The problem is urgent | User recalls repeated manual work after a recent process change | Recalled behaviour | Budget holder does not rank it among funded priorities | Test urgency with budget holders |
| The buyer can purchase | Budget holder describes annual planning | Reported process | No confirmed budget line for this category | Explore a funded use case |
| Entry is operationally feasible | Procurement stakeholder flags a local requirement | Unverified claim | Legal verification pending | Do not promise a launch date |

Triangulate market-scale claims separately: interview frequency is not a market-size estimate. [Bottom-up market sizing with customer interviews](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/bottom-up-market-sizing-customer-interviews/) can connect buyer evidence to a transparent sizing model. Map recurring needs to implications for the product, proposition, channel, sales process and further research.

## Complete the market-entry decision matrix

Set thresholds before fieldwork and retain evidence both for and against entry. The completed hypothetical matrix below shows the decision record to use.

| Decision condition | Pre-set threshold | Supporting evidence | Counter-evidence | Corroboration | Confidence | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem urgency | Two target segments report a recent costly trigger | Users report operational delays | One segment treats the issue as routine | Partial | Medium | Research lead |
| Current spend or workaround | Existing spend or a measurable workaround | Paid tools and manual administration reported | Spend belongs to another function | Partial | Medium | Commercial lead |
| Reachable buying process | Feasible route to budget and approval | Budget holders describe annual planning | Security review timing unclear | Partial | Low | Sales lead |
| Competitive opening | Defined weakness in current alternatives | Reporting and support friction reported | Incumbent contracts remain active | Partial | Medium | Product lead |
| Localisation burden | Required changes fit the pilot | Language needs identified | Local contracting requirement unverified | No | Low | Operations lead |
| Hard external constraints | No unresolved prohibition or critical delivery block | None established | Regulatory and contractual advice pending | No | Low | Legal owner |

Hard constraints override an attractive aggregate score. Clear demand does not justify entry if you cannot lawfully contract, deliver or support the offer.

| Recommendation | Named risk | Next test | Owner | Review date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| More research | Local contracting requirement remains unverified | Obtain qualified legal and contracting review | Legal owner | Before pilot approval |

Route the decision as follows:

- **Go:** demand and access are corroborated, with no critical constraint outstanding.
- **Limited pilot:** the opportunity is promising and remaining uncertainty can be tested within a bounded programme.
- **More research:** material buyer roles, segments or external checks are missing.
- **Pause:** a critical condition fails, or the required change is not commercially justified.

Use [message testing for value propositions](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/message-testing-value-propositions/) when the unresolved question is whether the proposition gives buyers a credible reason to change.

## Move from interview evidence to a bounded market test

Convert the recommendation into the smallest credible commercial test: qualified follow-up conversations, a procurement review, a paid pilot or a channel experiment. Define observable success and stop conditions before it starts.

Keep market-size estimation, proposition testing and purchase validation connected but distinct. An interview finding gives you a reason to test behaviour; it does not replace the test. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many buyer interviews are needed to validate a B2B market?

There is no universal number. Work in purposeful waves across material segments and buying roles, then review the gaps. Interviews provide directional qualitative evidence that should be corroborated before an entry commitment.

### Who should take part in market-entry interviews?

Include recent category evaluators across the buying system: users, problem owners, influencers, budget holders and relevant procurement, security, legal or channel roles.

### What are the best market-entry interview questions?

Focus on the last real purchase, its trigger, the consequence of inaction, alternatives considered, decision participants, budget route, approval barriers, localisation needs and the next credible commitment.

### Can buyer interviews prove willingness to pay?

No. They can reveal existing spend, budget ownership and pricing context, but hypothetical answers are weak evidence. Follow them with a behavioural test such as a paid pilot or procurement step.

### How should international customer interviews be translated?

Use a briefed, neutral interpreter or local moderator. Pilot translated terminology, record whether evidence is verbatim or translated, and independently verify consequential local claims.
