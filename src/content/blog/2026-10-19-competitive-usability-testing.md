## What competitive usability testing answers that a feature audit cannot

A feature audit shows what each product offers. Competitive usability testing shows whether people can achieve the same important goal, and where the experience breaks down.

It observes representative users attempting functionally equivalent goals in your product and realistic alternatives under documented conditions. Usability is an outcome of use in a specified context, rather than an absolute product quality ([ISO 9241-11](https://committee.iso.org/standard/63500.html)). The user segment, goal, device, data and starting point therefore matter to the comparison.

The decision is not which product has the longest feature list. It is whether to protect an advantage, close a meaningful gap, or avoid copying a feature that does not help users complete their work.

A feature matrix records availability. A heuristic review records expert judgement. A longitudinal benchmark tracks one product over time. Competitive usability testing measures people completing comparable goals across products. See our guide to [UX research methods](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/ux-research-methods/).

A small, directional study can reveal likely opportunity areas. Claims of reliable differences need a study designed and sized for that purpose.

## Choose competitors and goals before choosing screens

Start with the decision. Name the owner, target segment, product moment and action that will change if the evidence is clear. “Decide whether to redesign report creation for operations managers” is a useful decision. “See how competitors compare” is not.

Select alternatives that intended users genuinely consider. These may include a direct competitor, an established incumbent, a spreadsheet or a manual workaround. A well-known product is not automatically a relevant substitute.

Choose two to four high-value goals that every selected product can support. “Produce and share a weekly performance report” may be comparable across products. “Use Product B’s proprietary prediction feature” is not. Exclude tasks without goal parity.

Use [top-task analysis](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/top-task-analysis-ux-research/), support themes, sales objections and behavioural evidence to identify candidate goals. Record why each alternative and task belongs in scope. Read [competitor analysis without copying the wrong competitors](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/competitor-analysis-without-copying-the-wrong-competitors/) before treating a market list as a testing shortlist.

Before recruitment, write a scope statement covering the decision, segment, alternatives, goal and exclusions.

## Build a fair, comparable benchmark protocol

A fair usability benchmark study needs equivalent conditions, not identical clicks. Use this readiness gate before approving each task.

| Readiness gate | Check before fieldwork |
| --- | --- |
| Comparable user goal | Is the participant pursuing the same outcome in every product? |
| Neutral wording | Does the prompt avoid product labels, feature names and hints? |
| Matched starting state | Are accounts, permissions, seeded data and prior progress equivalent? |
| Pre-set success rule | Can success be classified without redefining it after sessions? |
| Rotation plan | Are product and task orders rotated where participants use more than one product? |

Write prompts around the outcome. “You need to prepare a report for a team meeting tomorrow. Find a way to create it and make it available to the team” is neutral. “Use the dashboard builder to publish a report” reveals a route.

Benchmark tasks should be believable and important to users, have a clear correct answer, and remain consistent when repeated ([GOV.UK Service Manual](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/measuring-success/usability-benchmarking-a-website-or-whole-service)). Products can use different interaction paths if they enable the same goal.

Define success, partial success, failure, abandonment and help rules before fieldwork. Use neutral, goal-led task wording and a consistent moderator guide: GOV.UK advises that tasks should not give away the answer, while a guide helps deliver them consistently ([GOV.UK guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing)).

Within-subjects testing, where each participant uses several products, can support direct comparison. Rotate product and task order to reduce practice and fatigue effects. Use between-subjects testing where familiarity with one product would distort later attempts.

Pilot the exact accounts, data, prompts, recordings and timing. Revise the protocol after the pilot, then freeze it for benchmark collection. Version later changes rather than mixing conditions. See [how to run a UX benchmarking study](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-to-run-a-ux-benchmarking-study/) and [prototype fidelity for usability testing](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/prototype-fidelity-for-usability-testing/).

## Measure performance and the reason behind the gap

Use a small scorecard that answers the decision. For each task, record:

- Task success: success, partial success, failure or abandonment.
- Time on successful attempts.
- Errors or wrong turns, defined before testing.
- A consistent post-task ease rating.
- An optional consistent post-study measure, such as the System Usability Scale (SUS), an established questionnaire ([Brooke’s SUS paper](https://hci-studies.org/methods-and-measures/downloads/SUS_Brooke1996.pdf)).

Interpret effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction together. A product can achieve high task success through lengthy workarounds. Another may feel quick to an experienced user while first-time users fail. A single winner score hides those distinctions.

Recruit for the intended segment and record familiarity with each product. Report the sample, segment, test date, environment, protocol exceptions and product versions where available. Familiarity is a limitation to disclose, not a reason to remove inconvenient results.

Pair metrics with observation. Capture hesitation points, participant strategy and neutral verbatim evidence to explain the performance result, not overwrite it. [Usability testing vs user interviews](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/usability-testing-vs-user-interviews/) explains why observed behaviour and stated preference answer different questions.

Report findings directionally: “Participants in this study found export more often on Product A” is more defensible than claiming Product A is universally easier.

A competitive UX analysis becomes useful when it changes a product decision. Use the worksheet below after each comparable task. Complete the readiness rows before fieldwork; complete the roadmap rows only when you have observed evidence. Score impact, confidence, effort and strategic differentiation consistently, for example from 1 to 5. The score supports discussion; it does not replace judgement.

| Task | Evidence gap | User consequence | Impact | Confidence | Effort | Strategic differentiation | Proposed action | Owner | Follow-up metric |

| Create and share a weekly performance report | Product A had lower observed task success than Products B and C; several participants missed sharing | The team cannot access the report before the meeting | 5 | 4 | 3 | 5 | Diagnose sharing discoverability, then test an alternative workflow | Reporting product lead | Task success and post-task ease |

Choose one response for each validated gap: close a harmful gap, protect an advantage, or deliberately do nothing when a competitor pattern does not serve the target user goal. For the highest-priority item, run a diagnostic test or [how to run a cognitive walkthrough](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-to-run-a-cognitive-walkthrough/) before committing to a solution. If the workflow includes automated assistance, consider [measuring agentic UX](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/measuring-agentic-ux/) alongside task measures.

Take a one-page heatmap, evidence appendix and named roadmap decision to the prioritisation workshop.

## Common mistakes that make competitor comparisons misleading

Poor controls turn competitor usability testing into opinion with timers attached.

Avoid too many products or tasks in one session. Avoid branded prompts, unmatched accounts or data, unequal moderator help and fixed product order. Do not call a feature gap a usability gap until users have pursued the same goal.

Do not compare a live competitor with an incomplete prototype as though conditions are equal. Missing states, placeholder content and lower reliability can create a product-condition effect rather than a UX finding. Disclose the limitation or change the study design.

Do not treat one score as proof of product strategy. Preserve recordings, observation notes, task definitions and protocol versions so the work can be audited or repeated. Strong [research operations](/blog/research-operations/) make this practical.

Before launch, confirm the decision owner and segment, realistic alternatives, goal-parity tasks, pre-declared success and help rules, a rotation plan, piloted accounts and recordings, a stable scorecard, and a prioritisation workshop.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is competitive usability testing?

It is a controlled study in which representative users attempt functionally equivalent goals across a product and realistic alternatives, using the same documented success definitions and measurement rules.

### How is competitive usability testing different from competitive UX analysis?

Competitive UX analysis can include feature inventories, expert reviews, pricing reviews and market positioning. Competitive usability testing specifically observes users completing comparable tasks and records performance and experience.

### How many competitors should a usability benchmark include?

Use a focused set of realistic alternatives that the target audience considers. Session length, participant fatigue and genuine task comparability, rather than a universal number, set the limit.

### Can you compare a prototype with a competitor’s live product?

Only with strong caveats and comparable fidelity for the workflow being tested. Unequal content, reliability, permissions or missing states can produce a product-condition effect rather than a UX finding.

### Which metrics matter in a competitive usability benchmark?

Use task success, time on successful attempts, errors or wrong turns, and a consistent post-task ease rating. Add a consistent post-study questionnaire where it supports the decision, and keep definitions stable across products.
