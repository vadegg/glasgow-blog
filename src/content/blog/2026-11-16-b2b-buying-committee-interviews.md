## The B2B buying committee interview method at a glance

B2B buying committee interviews turn separate recollections of one purchase into an evidence-based account of who influenced what, when and why. The unit of analysis is a specific decision in a specific account, not a generic persona or organisation chart.

Use this six-step method:

1. Define the purchase, time window and outcome.
2. Sample account-role cells deliberately.
3. Interview stakeholders separately.
4. Reconstruct each account’s decision sequence.
5. Map influence through observed actions.
6. Synthesise patterns and disagreements across accounts.

This differs from sales discovery, which aims to progress one opportunity. It also differs from project-planning stakeholder interviews and standalone buyer interviews, which may not reconstruct a shared decision. First, [choose the customer research method that matches the decision](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/customer-research-methods/). Use this approach when influence, disagreement and process are central.

The output is an account-by-role evidence matrix. It records actions, criteria, corroboration and conflicts before you create a cross-account committee map. For related methods, visit the [Product Research hub](/blog/product-research/).

## Build an account-by-role sampling frame

Define one purchase category, time window and decision outcome before recruiting. Treat new purchases, renewals and expansions as separate groups unless comparison is part of the research question. Their triggers, approval paths and evidence requirements may differ.

Create a sampling frame with accounts as rows and enacted buying roles as columns. Start with provisional roles such as initiator, champion, user, technical evaluator, risk reviewer, procurement, economic buyer and final approver. These are responsibilities to verify, not quotas based on job title. One person may hold several roles, and some purchases will not include every role.

Sample purposively: choose accounts with relevant variation in segment, size, outcome or purchase context. Within an account, seek contrasting perspectives. A champion and approver may agree on the outcome while describing different pressures, evidence and constraints.

Recruitment can begin with a known contact, but verify eligibility independently. Ask what the participant did, when they joined the decision and which action they could take. “I was involved” does not establish influence.

Continue recruiting while extra interviews add relevant variation in important account-role cells. GOV.UK describes continuing until additional interviews no longer produce new insights; use this as a stopping consideration, not a universal interview target ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/interview-study-qualitative-studies)). For planning research, see [stakeholder interviews for research planning](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/stakeholder-interviews-ux-research-planning/).

## Map influence as actions, not seniority

Map influence by purchase stage and observable action rather than title or seniority. Begin with a provisional map, then revise it after each interview.

Record who initiated the search, framed requirements, introduced a criterion, evaluated options, recommended a supplier, blocked progress, negotiated terms, approved spend, implemented the product or used it. Research on business purchasing found that influence depended on involvement, power relationships and circumstances, rather than role labels alone ([Journal of Business & Industrial Marketing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0885862419000919)).

Keep these forms of influence separate:

- Formal authority: ability to approve or reject.
- Informal persuasion: ability to shape others’ views.
- Technical gatekeeping: ability to set or test feasibility requirements.
- Process control: ability to control procurement, risk or approval steps.

Do not reduce these to a single numerical score. Interview evidence can show patterns of influence, but it rarely supports that level of precision.

Ask who changed the shortlist, delayed a decision, resolved disagreement or could have stopped the purchase. Seek process and risk perspectives as well as product specialists. Procurement, finance, legal and operations are described as “hidden buyers” because they bring different criteria to the decision ([LinkedIn B2B Institute and Bain](https://business.linkedin.com/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2b-research/trends/the-hidden-buyer-gap)).

Record the source and confidence for every relationship. A decision timeline can make changes between stages easier to see; [B2B customer journey mapping research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/b2b-customer-journey-mapping-research/) is a useful companion method.

## Interview each stakeholder with a shared event spine

Interview committee members separately when independent accounts of events, objections and influence matter. A group session can later test a shared process map, but hierarchy and conformity may conceal disagreement.

Use a shared event spine for every participant:

1. What triggered the purchase?
2. Who entered the process, and when?
3. Which criteria shaped the shortlist?
4. What objections or risks emerged?
5. Who approved, delayed or changed the decision?
6. What happened during implementation and after purchase?

Then add role-specific probes. Ask what the participant personally did, which evidence they required, what dependencies they managed and whether they could alter or stop the decision. Questions such as “What happened next?” and “What did you do after that?” help reconstruct behaviour more reliably than questions about an ideal buying process.

Ask every participant who else was involved, but do not reveal another participant’s account while testing inconsistencies. Ask instead for dates, meetings, documents, evaluation artefacts or approval records that may corroborate recalled events. Obtain appropriate consent before recording, retaining artefacts or using quotations.

A shared guide can evolve as new issues emerge while retaining enough consistency for comparison ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/interview-study-qualitative-studies)). Select the moderation format to suit the sensitivity and complexity of the decision; see [AI-moderated versus human-moderated research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/ai-moderated-vs-human-moderated-research/).

## Synthesise conflicting accounts without forcing consensus

Analyse each account as a case before comparing it with others. Reconstruct its sequence, context and outcome first. Cross-account patterns are more useful when they retain the conditions in which decisions happened.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Account A, selected supplier | A1 | Champion | Requirements | Added an integration requirement | Team adoption | Informal persuasion | Interview account | Evaluator recalled the requirement before shortlisting | Approver recalled price as the first filter | Complementary: criteria mattered at different stages | High |
| Account A, selected supplier | A2 | Technical evaluator | Evaluation | Removed an option after security review | Security assurance | Technical gatekeeping | Interview and review record | Champion recalled the shortlist changing | None | Corroborated event | High |
| Account B, delayed then selected supplier | B1 | Economic buyer | Approval | Paused approval pending a revised business case | Financial risk | Formal authority | Interview account | Procurement participant recalled the pause | Champion attributed the pause to procurement | Contradiction; approval record attributes the action to B1 | Medium |
| Account C, did not purchase | C1 | Procurement | Negotiation | Stopped contracting over terms | Contractual risk | Process control | Interview account | No second participant available | None | Unresolved: single-source account | Low |

Classify evidence as agreement, complementary perspective, factual contradiction or unresolved unknown. A champion may describe an evaluator as blocking progress while the evaluator describes the same action as reducing implementation risk. That can be a perspective difference rather than a disagreement about what happened.

For factual contradictions, compare timelines and artefacts, then seek another source where justified. Do not default to the most senior account. Rigorous qualitative analysis includes examining evidence that challenges the emerging explanation ([The BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/320/7226/50.1)).

## Turn the synthesis into an evidence-based committee map

Create two connected outputs: an account-level decision timeline showing participants, actions, criteria and turning points; and a cross-account role-by-stage pattern map.

For every conclusion, state:

- Which accounts and roles support it.
- Whether the evidence is direct or inferred.
- What evidence contradicts or limits it.
- Whether the pattern applies to a stage rather than every decision.

Report shared requirements separately from role-specific requirements. Gartner reports that role-tailored communication alone can impede a high-quality group decision, so retain both common decision needs and role-specific concerns ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/6385743)).

Translate findings into recruitment priorities, product requirements, evidence for content, sales enablement or service-design changes. Keep scope limits visible: flag thin role cells, overrepresented champions and findings drawn only from successful purchases.

Qualitative patterns explain mechanisms and variation. They do not estimate population prevalence. The next step is to [turn research insight into impact](/blog/insight-to-impact/) without overstating what the evidence supports.

## Quality check before sharing the findings

Before sharing the map, check that every claim names its account, role and evidence source. Separate observed actions, participant interpretations and researcher inferences.

- Have you checked for missing gatekeepers and hidden process roles?
- Have you retained contradictory and unresolved accounts?
- Have you tested job-title assumptions against observed actions?
- Have you redacted identities and commercially sensitive details consistently?
- Can another researcher trace each conclusion back to the matrix?

Apply the checklist to one completed account before making cross-account claims. Fix weak evidence links first.

## FAQs

### How many people should you interview from each buying committee?

There is no universal number. Seek contrasting perspectives within an account, prioritise roles central to the research question and continue across important account-role cells until further interviews add little relevant variation.

### Should buying committee members be interviewed together or separately?

Use separate interviews when independent accounts of influence, objections and events matter. A joint session can later test a shared process map, but hierarchy and conformity can suppress disagreement.

### How do you reconcile contradictory stakeholder interviews?

Classify the discrepancy, compare timelines and artefacts, seek a third source where justified, and retain unresolved differences. Do not treat the most senior participant’s account as correct by default.

### What is the difference between a job title and a buying role?

A job title describes organisational position. A buying role describes what someone did in a specific purchase. The same person can champion one decision, approve another or have no involvement at all.
