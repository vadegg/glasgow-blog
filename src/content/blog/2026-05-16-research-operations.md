Research operations is the work that makes repeated research possible: recruiting participants, handling consent and data, maintaining tools, and keeping findings usable. The goal is to remove recurring friction while protecting research quality.

## Start with the bottleneck

List the last three studies and the steps that caused avoidable delays. Separate access problems from study-design problems. A participant database will not fix an unclear research question, and another analysis tool will not repair missing consent.

[NN/g's ResearchOps guide](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/research-ops-study-guide/) provides an overview of this operational discipline. The ownership table below is a practical starting point to adapt to your team.

| Responsibility | Accountable role | Record to maintain | Before a study starts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Research scope | Study lead | Decision and questions | Someone can act on the findings |
| Recruitment | Recruitment owner | Criteria, channels, quotas and contact history | Required user groups can be reached |
| Consent and access | Study lead with the appropriate data owner | Agreed uses, access and retention | Participation and recording choices are understood |
| Tools | Workspace owner | Approved tools, permissions and export route | Recordings and exports work in a pilot |
| Evidence storage | Repository owner | Study index and links to restricted evidence | Readers can distinguish findings from raw data |
| Decisions | Product or service owner | Decision log and follow-up | There is a review date and an owner |

Small teams may give several responsibilities to one person. Keep the responsibilities visible even when the roles overlap.

## Make recruitment repeatable

Maintain only the participant information needed for a stated research purpose. Record relevant experience and contact history rather than treating a job title as proof of eligibility. Use [B2B recruitment guidance](/blog/recruiting-b2b-interview-participants/) and a [screener](/blog/screener-survey-best-practices-ux-research/) to qualify actual behaviour.

An opted-in panel can reduce repeated sourcing work, but it also creates selection and repeat-participation risks. Track invitations and past studies, make withdrawal easy, and use [panel management](/blog/ux-research-panel-management/) to keep the pool relevant. Do not promise a fixed recruiting speed or response rate.

## Treat consent as part of the workflow

Explain the purpose, recording choices, access and intended use before a session. Research participation does not imply permission to use someone's recording in public marketing. The [GOV.UK informed-consent guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/getting-users-consent-for-research) is a useful operational reference; organisational and jurisdictional requirements still need to be established for your study.

For AI-assisted recording or synthesis, check the actual service and agreement rather than relying on a generic claim about privacy. See [AI notetaker consent](/blog/ai-notetaker-consent-privacy-user-research/) and the [AI tool evaluation framework](/blog/how-to-choose-ai-user-research-tools/).

## A study launch checklist

- The research question could change a named decision.
- Selection criteria describe relevant experience, including excluded groups.
- Invitations, incentives and withdrawal procedures are documented.
- A pilot confirms that access, recording and exports work.
- Restricted evidence has an owner, an access policy and a retention decision.
- Findings have a review meeting and a route into the decision log.

After the study, record where the process stalled and fix that part before adding more tooling. Track elapsed recruiting time, avoidable cancellations and whether a decision was actually recorded; interpret these alongside study complexity. Use [research ROI](/blog/how-to-measure-ux-research-roi/) for the distinction between operational efficiency and business impact.
