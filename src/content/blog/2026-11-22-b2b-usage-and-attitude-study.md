## What a B2B usage and attitude study must decide

A **B2B usage and attitude study** quantifies category behaviour, brand usage, needs and attitudes in a defined business market. It should begin with the commercial decision it must inform, not a questionnaire template.

Used appropriately, usage and attitude research can support target selection, proposition priorities, channel strategy and portfolio investment. It measures buying patterns, product preferences and unmet needs, as described by [B2B International](https://www.b2binternational.com/what-we-do/markets/usage-attitude-research/). It is different from customer-satisfaction research, brand tracking, segmentation or exploratory buying-process interviews.

Choose another method when the market is not yet defined, the category language is unclear, or the real need is exploratory. Interviews, observation or [product research methods](/blog/product-research/) may be the right first step.

## Start with decisions, not a list of survey questions

Turn each stakeholder request into a decision, the evidence required and the action threshold. “Understand perceptions” is not a decision. “Decide whether to prioritise enterprise IT teams or operations teams” is.

Use a decision-to-measure chain:

| Decision | Population | Measure | Comparison | Planned action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prioritise a target segment | Eligible accounts | Category use and unmet need | Segment and company size | Focus proposition and outreach |
| Refine a proposition | Relevant buying roles | Importance and perceived performance | Users and non-users | Change message or product priority |
| Choose a channel | Recent buying participants | Information sources and influence | Role and buying stage | Allocate channel investment |

The completed matrix below is a hypothetical study specification for a vertical SaaS provider in a known 250-account market. It is a design example, not a study result.

| Business decision | Unit of analysis | Eligible roles | Category boundary | Required comparison | Sampling source | Quota or census approach | Questionnaire module | Analysis output | Decision rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prioritise account segments | Account | Operational owner or commercial lead | Organisations in the 250-account market that evaluated or used relevant software in the past 24 months | Industry and account size | Verified account list, customer records and direct outreach | Census invitation; reporting bases agreed before fieldwork | Screening, category use and needs | Account-level need and usage profile | Prioritise only segments with enough completed accounts and a consistent need pattern |
| Improve product proposition | Account and respondent | Primary users, technical evaluators and economic buyers | Current users and recent evaluators | Role and usage status | Deduplicated account list and direct outreach | Census; role targets within selected accounts | Workflows, needs, attitudes and value | Role-matched evidence table | Change the proposition only where the relevant role supports the finding |
| Review acquisition route | Buying event | Participants in a purchase or renewal in the past 12 months | Recent category decisions | Won, lost and retained accounts | Customer records, prospect list and direct outreach | Census of known contactable events | Buying process, sources and influence | Stage-by-stage influence map | Fund channels linked to the stages they influence |

An interesting difference is not automatically actionable. It needs an adequate base, a relevant comparison and a named decision owner. This is the discipline behind [turning research findings into action](/blog/insight-to-impact/).

## Define the market, account and respondent correctly

Write inclusion and exclusion rules before recruitment. Define the category, geography, company size, industry and recent category involvement. State whether subsidiaries count separately and whether the study covers customers, prospects or both.

Choose the unit of analysis first: enterprise, establishment, account, buying event or individual. Business-survey documentation from the [World Bank Group](https://microdata.worldbank.org/index.php/catalog/6709/study-description) illustrates the need to specify a primary sampling unit; establishments can also be stratified by characteristics such as industry, size and region.

The respondent is not always the analysis unit. A user may be best placed to report workflow, frequency and practical problems. A technical evaluator may report integration and security criteria. An economic buyer may report approval routes, investment priorities and value perceptions.

Do not treat several respondents from one account as independent account observations. Retain account and respondent identifiers, define how responses from the same organisation will be handled, and identify which measures remain person-level.

One knowledgeable informant can be sufficient for a clearly account-wide fact. Recruit role-matched respondents where different perspectives affect the decision. Use the role map in [sampling a B2B buying committee](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/b2b-buying-committee-interviews/) rather than relying on a generic “decision-maker” screen.

## Build a defensible sample from a small customer universe

Small B2B markets are often a coverage problem before they are a sample-size problem. Inventory each available frame separately: customer records, prospect lists, partner lists, trade associations, specialist panels and direct outreach.

Assess each frame for coverage, duplication, stale records, evidence of eligibility and likely overrepresentation. A sampling frame is the information used to contact potential respondents, according to [AAPOR](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/). Reconcile overlapping frames at account level before sending invitations.

For a known small universe, compare census outreach with stratified sampling. Stratify only on variables needed for decisions, such as segment, size, usage status or role. Excessive quotas can make recruitment impractical and leave too little data in each subgroup.

Set feasibility ranges after reviewing incidence and contactability rather than promising a universal sample size. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/05/08/when-surveying-small-populations-some-approaches-are-more-inclusive-than-others/) notes that meaningful samples from small populations can be difficult and expensive, and that opt-in approaches can attract false claimants.

Quotas can improve sample composition; they do not make an opt-in sample a probability sample. Build in employer and role checks, category-knowledge questions, duplicate-account controls and review of implausible response patterns. See [detecting fake survey respondents](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-to-detect-fake-survey-respondents/).

In the hypothetical 250-account market, if only 40 accounts are large, invite all 40 rather than promise a precise large-account subgroup result. Report the achieved base and qualify findings when the completed base cannot support the intended comparison.

## Design the B2B brand usage survey

Build a **B2B brand usage survey** in the order respondents can answer reliably:

1. Screening and account eligibility
2. Category behaviour and recent buying activity
3. Current and previous usage
4. Buying roles and influence by stage
5. Needs, barriers and attitudes
6. Brand awareness, consideration and use
7. Switching and retention
8. Firmographics and account classification

Use a defined reference period. Distinguish unaided awareness, aided awareness, consideration, trial, current use, primary use and discontinued use. A single “Have you used this?” question cannot make those distinctions.

Keep personal behaviour separate from organisation-level behaviour. Ask which tools a respondent personally uses separately from which tools their organisation uses. Include “I do not know” and “I was not involved” where knowledge is partial.

Measure influence by stage: identifying a need, setting requirements, evaluating options, approving spend, selecting a supplier and implementation. This is more informative than one broad decision-maker label.

Ask unaided awareness before displaying a brand list. Rotate long brand or attribute lists where appropriate. Pretest wording, category language, recall burden, role interpretation and answer options with eligible respondents. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/) describes pretesting as an essential part of questionnaire design and notes that wording, order and mode can affect responses. Use [cognitive interviewing for survey pretesting](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/cognitive-interviewing-survey-pretesting/) alongside [survey design best practices](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/survey-design-best-practices-ux/).

## Analyse small samples without overstating certainty

Predefine account-level and person-level outputs, minimum reporting bases and the treatment of multiple respondents from one organisation. Report descriptive estimates with visible bases, and avoid ranking small subgroups on minor percentage differences.

Weight only where credible population information exists. Disclose the weighting variables, source, trimming and likely effect on precision. AAPOR’s [Transparency Initiative](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/transparency-initiative/) calls for clear disclosure, including whether data were weighted.

For nonprobability samples, describe selection limits instead of presenting a conventional margin of error that the design cannot justify. Weighting may align a sample with known account characteristics, but cannot resolve unknown coverage gaps or self-selection.

Plan comparisons around role, usage status and company characteristics. Statistical segmentation is not a default output for a small B2B U&A study: it needs suitable variables, adequate effective sample and validation. Apply [survey weighting and raking](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/survey-weighting-raking-market-research/) carefully, and assess whether [customer segmentation research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-to-run-a-customer-segmentation-research-study/) is feasible before committing to a segment solution.

Each output should state the decision it informs, the evidence strength and any follow-up research required.

A field-ready design has:

- A named decision owner and planned action for every primary output.
- A clear market definition, account rule and unit of analysis.
- Role eligibility matched to the knowledge required.
- Separate, deduplicated frames and feasible quotas or census targets.
- A pretest, respondent-quality controls and a plan for incomplete knowledge.
- A documented weighting decision and transparent reporting limits.

Proceed when the design can answer named decisions with reachable respondents. Revise it when essential comparisons lack a plausible route to enough eligible accounts. Stop when the market, category or decision is still too unclear for quantitative research.

Scope the market and test recruitment feasibility before committing to fieldwork.
