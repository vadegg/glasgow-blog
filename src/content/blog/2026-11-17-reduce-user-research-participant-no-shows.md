## Start with a useful definition of a no-show

A missed session costs a research slot. Before trying to reduce no-shows, define attendance outcomes consistently so you can see where the workflow fails.

Use these labels:

- **Booked:** a participant has selected a session.
- **Confirmed:** they have acknowledged the practical details.
- **Cancelled with notice:** they withdrew early enough for you to act.
- **Late cancellation:** they withdrew after the replacement window began.
- **No-show:** they did not attend and gave no notice within the agreed window.
- **Replacement:** a participant recruited or activated to fill a lost slot.
- **Completed session:** a session that produced usable research.

Calculate the participant no-show rate as:

`No-show rate = no-shows ÷ booked participants × 100`

Report cancellations separately. Combining cancellations and no-shows makes it harder to tell whether the issue is reminder timing, joining instructions or short-notice diary changes.

Track recruitment source, session format, invite-to-session lead time, participant segment and reminder channel. This makes attendance a research operations question rather than a judgement about participants. See [research operations](/blog/research-operations/) for the wider workflow.

For example, an illustrative tracker with 12 bookings, 10 completed sessions, one cancellation with notice and one no-show has an 8.3% no-show rate. The cancellation is recorded separately.

## Make confirmation an active commitment, not a calendar event

Send confirmation as soon as a participant is eligible and booked. The Market Research Society advises prompt confirmation communications and asks participants to let researchers know as soon as possible if they cannot attend ([MRS](https://rtp.mrs.org.uk/course-entry/id/1762)).

A calendar invitation is useful, but it does not show that someone has read the details. Request a simple acknowledgement, such as “Yes, I can attend”, or offer another accessible way to confirm. Follow up where acknowledgement is missing without treating silence as agreement.

Keep the message short and put practical details where they are easy to find on a phone:

- date, start time and time zone;
- expected duration and format;
- meeting link, address or travel instructions;
- preparation, device or access requirements;
- recording and consent information;
- a named contact for support;
- a clear cancellation or rescheduling route; and
- the incentive amount, form and expected timing.

Make cancelling straightforward. A reply address or cancellation link gives the participant a workable route and gives your team more time to respond.

State that the incentive compensates time and agreed costs, not favourable views or successful task completion. For recruitment foundations, read [how to recruit participants for user research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-to-recruit-participants-for-user-research/).

## Build reminders around attendance barriers

Use research session reminders to remove the next practical barrier, rather than repeatedly sending the full briefing. A useful starting sequence is confirmation at booking, a day-before check-in and a day-of joining prompt. UXQB includes a 24-hour call or SMS as a contingency measure for important usability sessions ([UXQB](https://uxqb.org/public/documents/CPUX-UT_EN_Curriculum.pdf)). Adapt the timing to the session and participant context.

Repeat only what is useful at each point:

- **Confirmation:** date, time, format, incentive and cancellation route.
- **Day before:** attendance check, joining or travel details, and support contact.
- **Day of:** start time, direct meeting link and a simple way to report a problem.

Ask during recruitment for the participant’s preferred workable contact channel. Email may suit one person; phone or text may be more accessible or reliable for another. Record the preference and use details only for the purposes explained to the participant.

For remote sessions, put the meeting link near the top of the day-of message. Explain any relevant device, browser or connection expectations and provide a support contact who can resolve access problems. Accessibility should shape the contact workflow from the start; our guide on how to [recruit disabled participants for accessibility research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/recruit-disabled-participants-accessibility-research/) explains this in more detail.

## Set an incentive policy that supports attendance fairly

Set incentives around the participant group, time, effort, specialist knowledge and participation costs. There is no universal amount: the Department for Education says there is no standard monetary incentive value and advises considering the audience, study demands and travel ([Department for Education](https://user-research.education.gov.uk/guidance/incentives-26/5)). GOV.UK also links the amount to participant type and session length ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/find-user-research-participants)).

A short remote session with a broad audience may need different arrangements from an in-person study involving travel, caring commitments, assistive support or specialist expertise.

In the participant confirmation process, explain:

1. the incentive amount or arrangement;
2. how it will be supplied, such as a voucher or bank transfer; and
3. when the participant should expect it.

Plan expenses and reasonable participation support where they make attendance possible. Ask only for the information needed to provide that support. Keep payment separate from research outcomes: participants should not be paid according to whether they complete a task quickly or provide views the team prefers.

A documented policy helps recruiters, researchers and finance teams give consistent answers. Use the [UK user research participant incentive framework](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/user-research-participant-incentives-uk/) to structure it.

## Protect the research plan when someone cancels or misses

Agree the recovery route before fieldwork begins. A repeatable process protects the research plan while treating participants fairly:

1. The participant signals a conflict through the stated route.
2. The researcher offers rescheduling where appropriate.
3. The team activates a reserve or opens a replacement slot if needed.
4. The outcome is recorded in the attendance tracker.

For critical usability sessions, UXQB advises considering a reserve participant, or “floater” ([UXQB](https://uxqb.org/public/documents/CPUX-UT_EN_Curriculum.pdf)). A reserve is not necessary for every study. Use one where the session’s importance, recruitment difficulty and budget justify it. Explain standby arrangements in advance and apply the communicated compensation terms fairly.

At session time, use the documented contact route and wait only for the pre-agreed window. Keep personal details out of observer channels and project spaces that do not need them.

After a missed session, record the outcome accurately, protect participant data and decide whether the completed sample still answers the research question. UserTesting distinguishes between replacing a no-show and adjusting the requested participant total ([UserTesting](https://help.usertesting.com/hc/en-us/articles/35841423355165-Replace-a-no-show)). Do not recruit a replacement automatically.

A maintained panel can make recovery easier. See [build and manage a user research panel](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/build-manage-user-research-panel/).

## Use the No-Show Prevention and Recovery Worksheet

Use this worksheet for every study. It creates a single record of prevention actions, attendance outcomes and recovery decisions. The completed entries below are illustrative workflow settings, not agency performance data.

| Field | Completed worksheet entry |
|---|---|
| Study and segment | Checkout usability study; existing mobile customers |
| Session format | Remote, 45 minutes |
| Booked / completed / no-show | 6 / record after fieldwork / record after fieldwork |
| Baseline-rate calculator | `(no-shows ÷ booked) × 100`; compare only with similar sessions |
| Invite-to-session lead time | 5 days |
| Confirmation status | Confirmation sent at booking; reply acknowledgement requested |
| Preferred contact channel | Ask and record during recruitment |
| Reminder schedule | Day-before check-in; day-of joining prompt |
| Access needs | Record agreed support needed for participation only |
| Incentive arrangement | Amount, form and timing stated in confirmation |
| Cancellation reason | Notice, late notice, unknown or rescheduled |
| Reserve decision rule | Use a reserve only if the session is critical, recruitment is difficult and budget is approved |
| Recovery action | Reschedule, activate reserve, recruit replacement or revise target |
| Owner | Named researcher |
| Post-session incident log | Outcome, contact attempt, recovery decision and rationale |

Operate the worksheet in five steps:

1. Establish a baseline no-show rate for comparable recent sessions.
2. Identify the segment or workflow stage with the clearest attendance friction.
3. Change one element, such as acknowledgement follow-up or the day-of prompt.
4. Run the next comparable wave and record the same fields.
5. Compare rates, then keep, revise or stop the change.

Before fieldwork, check that each participant has practical details, a cancellation route, incentive terms and an appropriate contact option. Confirm reserve arrangements, wait windows and decision ownership with the team.

Participants must remain free to decline, cancel or reschedule without giving a reason. Limit the worksheet to information needed to run the study and restrict access to people who need it. For the surrounding operating model, see [user research panel management](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/ux-research-panel-management/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a participant no-show rate in user research?

It is the number of no-shows divided by booked participants for a stated study period. Track cancellations with notice separately so they do not conceal attendance problems.

### When should I remind user research participants?

Use immediate confirmation, a day-before reminder and a day-of joining prompt as a starting workflow. Adapt timing and channel to the participant’s needs, session format and practical barriers.

### Should I over-recruit for user research sessions?

Use reserves or extra capacity as a deliberate decision. Critical sessions, difficult recruitment and observed attendance patterns may justify them; routine over-recruitment can add cost and create a poor participant experience.

### Should participants receive an incentive if they do not attend?

It depends on the terms communicated and the circumstances. State the policy before booking, apply it consistently and make reserve arrangements fair.

For your next study, complete the worksheet before recruitment begins, then use its incident log to decide which part of the attendance workflow to improve.
