Most B2B purchases are settled by a committee of six to ten people, and you will never speak to most of them. Aim your messaging at one persona and your demo at one evaluator, and everyone else is a guess. What follows is a step-by-step B2B buyer research process for swapping that guesswork for evidence.

## What is the B2B buyer research process?

It is a repeatable programme for learning three things: who is involved in a purchase, what each person cares about, and how the decision actually gets made. Think of it as a standing capability, not a one-off study.

B2C research can often centre on a single shopper. B2B cannot. A typical purchase runs through a buying committee — the group who evaluate, veto and approve it — of six to ten people or more. The choice is reached by consensus rather than a single yes, and it passes through security, legal and procurement gates. Cycles stretch six to eighteen months, and most of the buyer's research happens before they ever contact a sales rep.

Guessing is expensive. It produces messaging aimed at one persona, demos that skip the technical evaluator, deals lost to "no decision", and roadmap bets placed on whichever customer shouts loudest.

Done properly, the process gives you three deliverables: a buying committee map, a research-backed buyer journey, and a weighted decision-criteria matrix. Run it as one strand of a wider [product research programme](/blog/product-research) that feeds marketing, sales enablement and the roadmap. The six steps below run in order — skim to the one you need.

## Step 1: Define the decisions your research must inform

Start with the choices that hinge on understanding buyers, not with a method. List the go-to-market and product decisions in play: positioning, ideal customer profile, target roles, the content plan, the sales playbook, packaging and roadmap sequencing.

Turn those into five to eight research questions. Who starts the search, and what triggers it? Who can veto a purchase? Which criteria decide the shortlist, and which decide the final pick? Where do deals stall? Why do we lose to a named competitor, and why do we lose to "do nothing"?

Agree who will use each finding, and how, before any fieldwork begins. Give every question a decision owner and a target decision date. This framing is what separates a repeatable process from scattered advice on how to research B2B buyers: begin from [the decisions research should inform](/blog/insight-to-impact) and work backwards.

## Step 2: Map the buying committee

Draft a first version from data you already hold, then plan to test it. Start with the roles that recur across B2B deals:

- **Initiator or champion** — feels the problem and pushes for change
- **Economic buyer** — signs off on budget
- **Technical and security evaluator** — checks it works and is safe to deploy
- **End users** — live with the tool day to day
- **Procurement** — negotiates terms and price
- **Legal** — reviews contracts, data handling and risk
- **Blockers** — anyone with a reason to keep the status quo

One person often holds several of these roles, especially in smaller companies. Record formal authority and informal influence separately: note who each role reports to, and what winning them concretely requires.

Pull from the usual sources for buying committee mapping: CRM opportunity contacts, sales and customer success debriefs, call-recording review, LinkedIn and org charts, and one interview prompt — "walk me through everyone who touched this decision." Give the map these columns: role, title, goals, success metrics, likely objections, evidence they trust, information sources, and level of influence.

Build a separate map per segment and deal size. Enterprise deals add security and procurement gates that mid-market and SMB deals skip. Treat this first map as a hypothesis for the Step 4 interviews to confirm or correct, and see the methods guide on [identifying who actually buys](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/b2b-buyer-research-methods).

## Step 3: Match research methods to buyer questions

Each buyer question has one method that answers it well and several that answer it poorly. Match them before you start fieldwork.

| Method | What it answers | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Win-loss interviews | Why you win and lose, shortlist criteria, competitive perception, deal-breakers | Outcomes from recent, real evaluations |
| JTBD (jobs to be done) or switch interviews | The trigger event; the push, pull, anxiety and habit forces; the timeline from first thought to purchase | Reconstructing why and when buyers move |
| Buyer journey mapping research | Stages, tasks, questions, content consumed, friction and hand-offs between roles | Seeing the decision as a process, not a moment |
| Buyer survey | Committee size and composition, criteria ranked by role | Quantifying what interviews suggest |
| Sales and customer success call recordings | Recurring objections, the language buyers use, where conversations stall | Cheap, continuous signal with no extra recruitment |
| Community, analyst, search and AI-assistant listening | How buyers build the initial shortlist before contacting you | Mapping the research buyers do alone |

Sequence for depth first, then breadth. Run [win-loss analysis](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/win-loss-analysis-b2b-saas) and switch interviews to understand the mechanics, add a survey to size the proportions, then layer in call-recording analysis for ongoing coverage. Listening research shows [how B2B buyers use AI to shortlist vendors](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-b2b-buyers-use-ai-to-research-vendors) and which sources shape the longlist.

Good purchase-decision research triangulates every important finding across at least two sources. Never lean on a single method, or on one committee member's account of how the choice was made. These are the core buying committee research methods; the deep-dive guides cover each in full.

## Step 4: Recruit and interview buyers across roles and outcomes

Recruit for recency and for range. Focus on deals closed in the last three to six months, while memory is fresh. Cover wins, losses and no-decisions, with two to three roles per deal where you can reach them, plus a few churned accounts for the full lifecycle view.

Aim for 12 to 20 interviews across six to eight deals to begin with. Recruit customers through your CRM and customer success team. For losses and non-customers, use a neutral third party and an incentive. Losses and no-decisions hold the most useful feedback and the most bias risk — buyers soften their reasons when they think they are talking to the company they turned down, so an interviewer with no stake in the outcome hears the blunt version. Our notes on [participant recruitment](/blog/research-operations) cover screening and scheduling in detail.

Screen for a confirmed role in a real evaluation within the timeframe, the right seniority, and the right segment.

A combined discussion guide can follow one arc: context and trigger, then the search and shortlist, then who was involved and who decided, then criteria and trade-offs, then the competitive comparison, then the decision moment and any friction, then what would have changed the outcome, and finally the post-purchase reality.

Anchor memory to events with the switch-interview timeline technique — work backwards from the purchase date through each prior step. Ask for artefacts too: evaluation scorecards and internal business cases, where buyers are allowed to share them.

## Step 5: Synthesise findings into three artefacts

Coding the transcripts — labelling each passage by theme, role and journey stage — turns them into three working documents.

**The validated buying committee map.** Populate every role from interview evidence — goals, objections, the proof each person needs, and real influence — with notes on how the picture shifts by segment.

**The research-backed buyer journey.** Lay out the stages — trigger, explore, evaluate, justify, purchase, onboard, expand — and for each one record the buyer's questions, the content and channels used, the emotional highs and lows, the drop-off points, and which roles are active.

**The weighted decision-criteria matrix.** List criteria by role, attach an importance weight, score how buyers perceive you against each competitor, and note the proof buyers actually accept rather than the proof you wish they would.

Once passages are tagged, look for patterns that repeat across three or more deals. Keep recurring signal separate from single anecdotes, and record the disconfirming cases explicitly so the artefacts stay honest.

In a buyer research programme we ran for a cross-border payments provider, B2B clients in every segment ranked reliability and predictability above price. One finance lead put it plainly: a payment promised for 11am had to arrive at 11am, or he was the one apologising to a supplier. That moved the messaging from exchange rates to settlement certainty. This is the point where buyer journey research becomes a decision tool rather than a diagram; see [B2B customer journey mapping research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/b2b-customer-journey-mapping-research) for the journey artefact in depth.

## Step 6: Turn buyer insight into GTM and product decisions

The artefacts are inputs to decisions, not the end of the work. Take each one to the owner and date you set in Step 1.

**Messaging.** Write one value message and one proof point per committee role, straight from the criteria matrix. The champion, the economic buyer and the security evaluator should each find a claim that answers their question.

**Content and demand.** Build one asset for each buyer question at each journey stage, aimed at the role who asks it. Gaps in the journey map are your content backlog.

**Sales enablement.** Add discovery questions that surface the whole committee early, objection-handling notes in the buyer's own words, and battlecards — one-page competitor comparisons — drawn from win-loss findings.

**Roadmap.** Sequence work against the criteria where buyers rate you weakest and care most, and against the friction points in the journey. A feature one vocal customer wants is not automatically a priority: in a journey study we ran for a diagnostics provider, a service option a handful of customers pushed for turned out to interest about 1% of the base.

**Packaging and pricing.** Align tiers with the economic buyer's success metrics and with the gates procurement and legal impose.

Then close the loop. Review the artefacts each quarter, refresh a handful of interviews as the market shifts, and check that the decisions you made are holding up.

## Start with the deals you already have

Pull ten recent closed-won and closed-lost opportunities from your CRM. List every contact attached to each, then sketch a first buying committee map from the deal notes and call recordings. The gaps in that sketch — the roles you cannot name, the criteria you are guessing at, the losses you cannot explain — are your interview plan for Step 4. Book the first three conversations before the end of the month.
