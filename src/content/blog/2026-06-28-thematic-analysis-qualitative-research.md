Thematic analysis develops an account of patterns of meaning in qualitative data. It is a family of approaches, not a single procedure that becomes rigorous by adding labels to transcripts. State which approach you are using and keep your analysis consistent with it.

## Choose the approach before choosing a coding workflow

[Braun and Clarke distinguish reflexive thematic analysis from other approaches](https://www.thematicanalysis.net/understanding-ta/). In reflexive TA, the researcher's interpretation is part of the analysis. A fixed codebook, independent-coder agreement and a consensus score are not interchangeable requirements for that approach.

| Analytic choice | What to make explicit |
|---|---|
| Reflexive thematic analysis | Your question, interpretive position, engagement with the data and development of themes |
| Codebook-based team analysis | How the coding framework is developed, used and revised; how analysts handle differences |
| Descriptive content categorisation | The categories, units and counting rules; what those counts can and cannot mean |

Do not label every spreadsheet of comments “Braun–Clarke analysis”. A practical team codebook can be useful without presenting it as a requirement of reflexive TA. Their [editor and reviewer guidance](https://www.thematicanalysis.net/editor-checklist/) identifies this methodological mismatch.

## Work through six phases iteratively

The authors describe familiarisation, coding, developing initial themes, reviewing and developing themes, refining and naming them, and writing the analysis. Their [guide to doing reflexive TA](https://www.thematicanalysis.net/doing-reflexive-ta/) treats the process as recursive: interpretation can send you back to earlier material.

For a product study, keep the research question visible as you work. Record why a theme explains a meaningful pattern, which extracts support it and what challenges it. A heading such as “onboarding” names a topic; it does not yet explain what is happening.

## A worked extract-to-theme example

The following extracts are **invented for teaching**. They are not participant quotations or client findings.

| Example extract | Initial code | Analytic question |
|---|---|---|
| “I saved it, but I still sent a screenshot to my manager to check.” | Seeking confirmation after completion | What makes completion feel uncertain? |
| “The green tick tells me the file arrived, not whether somebody approved it.” | Distinguishing receipt from approval | Which system states do people need to tell apart? |
| “I did not need approval; I only wanted to know where the file went.” | Needing traceability rather than authorisation | Does the proposed interpretation fit different circumstances? |

A possible candidate theme is **completion without a clear next state creates verification work**. It connects the extracts more specifically than “trust” or “feedback”. However, the third extract challenges an interpretation framed solely around approval. A real analysis would return to the full dataset and the study context before settling on a theme.

Download the [analysis worksheet](/downloads/thematic-analysis-worksheet.csv) to keep extracts, codes, interpretive notes and contrary evidence together. Its fields support an audit trail; completing them does not validate the interpretation automatically.

## Keep frequency, importance and uncertainty separate

A theme is not necessarily important because its label occurs most often. Several extracts from one participant do not equal independent accounts from several people. In a purposively recruited interview sample, counts do not estimate market prevalence.

Use a [participant-by-evidence matrix](/blog/how-to-analyze-user-interview-data/) if comparison across cases helps answer the question. State how the matrix relates to your analytic approach, and avoid reducing a nuanced interpretation to a vote.

## Use AI assistance with traceable evidence

AI may help locate candidate passages or suggest alternative readings. Check every extract against the original, review context and record the analyst's decisions. A generated theme name does not demonstrate that a pattern exists in the dataset.

For tool choices, see [AI tools for qualitative research](/blog/ai-tools-for-qualitative-research/). For verification, use [validating AI-generated insights](/blog/validate-ai-generated-research-insights/). Neither replaces methodological judgement or the study's consent and data arrangements.

## Write findings that support a decision

For each finding, present the interpretive claim, evidence, contrary cases and scope. Separate what the study supports from the product response you propose. A theme about verification work might motivate several designs, not just an approval banner.

Use [affinity mapping](/blog/affinity-mapping-in-qualitative-research/) for organising material when appropriate, and [insight to impact](/blog/insight-to-impact/) to make the evidence-to-decision chain inspectable. This page covers thematic analysis across qualitative product and UX research; a second general coding guide is not needed for the same task.
