UX research methods help you understand people, observe how they use a product and evaluate possible changes. Start with a decision and an uncertainty; then choose the evidence that could change your mind.

## Choose a method by the question

Interviews reveal accounts and explanations; observing a task reveals behaviour in that setting. Neither automatically estimates how common a problem is. This distinction between attitudinal and behavioural evidence is central to [NN/g's research-methods framework](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/which-ux-research-methods/).

| Your uncertainty | Useful starting method | Evidence to collect | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Why people adopt or abandon a workflow | [User interviews](/blog/how-to-conduct-user-interviews/) | Recent events, alternatives and constraints | Population prevalence or actual task performance |
| Whether someone can complete a task | [Usability testing](/blog/usability-testing-vs-user-interviews/) | Success, errors, workarounds and recovery | Why the wider market would buy |
| How work happens outside your interface | [Contextual inquiry](/blog/contextual-inquiry-user-research/) | Tools, interruptions and dependencies | Behaviour in settings you did not observe |
| Whether labels and structure make sense | [Card sorting](/blog/card-sorting-ux-research/) and [tree testing](/blog/tree-testing-ux-research/) | Groupings, paths and mistaken destinations | Complete usability of the rendered interface |
| How responses vary across a defined audience | [Survey design](/blog/survey-design-best-practices-ux/) | Answers from a documented sampling process | Causation or representativeness of an opt-in sample |
| Whether an experience changes over time | [Diary studies](/blog/diary-study-ux-research/) | Repeated episodes and changing circumstances | Events participants omit or cannot recall |

Use [cognitive walkthroughs](/blog/how-to-run-a-cognitive-walkthrough/) or [heuristic evaluation](/blog/heuristic-evaluation-ux-research/) to inspect a design before recruiting. Treat the resulting issues as expert predictions to investigate, rather than participant findings.

## A worked study plan

Consider a hypothetical invoice product whose users often leave during account setup. The decision is whether to redesign the permissions step or change the information shown before it.

| Planning field | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Decision owner | Product manager responsible for activation |
| Uncertainty | Users may not understand the requested access, or may lack authority to grant it |
| Participants | People who recently tried setup, including those who stopped and those who completed it |
| First activity | Ask about the recent attempt; observe setup with safe test data |
| Evidence record | Step, action, explanation, permission available, consequence and recovery |
| Possible decisions | Improve the explanation; add an administrator handoff; investigate a different barrier |
| Follow-up | Retest the changed step, then monitor completion alongside changes in audience and acquisition |

This is an example plan, not a reported client result. A participant who cannot grant access has a different problem from someone who cannot find the button. Keep those observations separate in the [research report](/blog/how-to-write-a-ux-research-report/).

## Set the sample and stopping rule

For exploratory work, begin with the user groups and tasks that could lead to different decisions. Review what remains unanswered after each round. For a quantitative comparison, plan around the effect or precision you need and the sampling method; do not borrow a universal participant count from a qualitative study.

Record exclusions, recruitment channels and missing groups. A convenient panel of current enthusiasts may miss people who abandoned the product. Use a [screener](/blog/screener-survey-best-practices-ux-research/) and plan [inclusive sessions](/blog/inclusive-ux-research-methods/) before recruitment begins.

## Combine methods when the evidence needs it

An interview can suggest a reason for abandonment. A usability session can expose a recoverability problem. A subsequent experiment can test whether a specific change improves a measured outcome. These are complementary claims, not interchangeable proof.
