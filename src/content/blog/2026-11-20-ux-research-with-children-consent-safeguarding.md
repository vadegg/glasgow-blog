## When UX research with children is justified

Use UX research with children when a product decision directly concerns their experience and adults cannot answer the question reliably on their behalf. Define the UK-focused scope first, then involve legal, data-protection, ethics and local safeguarding reviewers where the study or setting requires it.

Ask three questions:

- Does the decision directly affect children?
- Can existing evidence, adult perspectives or a lower-risk method answer it?
- Would excluding children leave the team relying on assumptions about their behaviour, language or needs?

Adults can provide valuable context, but they cannot always explain what a child understands, notices or finds difficult. Build this decision into your [research operations](/blog/research-operations/) rather than leaving it to the moderator on the day.

The worksheet below produces one of three outcomes: **proceed** when controls are ready for review; **revise** when gaps can be resolved; or **stop and escalate** when specialist advice is needed.

## Consent, assent and data consent are different decisions

Parental or guardian permission, child assent and data-protection decisions are separate. Combining them in one form can obscure what each person has agreed to.

Parental or guardian permission is agreement from someone with appropriate responsibility for the child. Child assent is the child’s affirmative, informed and voluntary willingness to take part. Informed consent requires an understanding of what is being agreed to, including relevant choices and consequences.

Government guidance says researchers should obtain informed agreement from both the child and a parent, guardian, carer or other responsible adult ([Government Digital Service](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/getting-users-consent-for-research)). UKRI also advises treating consent as a dialogue with children and parents ([UK Research and Innovation](https://www.ukri.org/councils/esrc/guidance-for-applicants/research-ethics-guidance/research-with-children-and-young-people/)).

A child’s willingness must continue throughout the session. Adult permission does not justify carrying on after refusal, distress or a request to stop. Explain the following in language the child can understand:

- why the research is happening and what they will do;
- who will be present or observing;
- whether audio, video or screens will be recorded;
- how information will be used and the limits of confidentiality; and
- how to skip, pause or stop.

Agreement to participate is separate from the organisation’s lawful basis for processing personal data. Outside particular online-service rules, the UK GDPR does not set a general minimum age at which a child can consent to processing their own data. Capacity and context matter, and consent may not be the appropriate lawful basis ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/children-and-the-uk-gdpr/how-do-the-lawful-bases-apply-to-children-s-personal-information/)).

Do not use 13 as a universal research-consent threshold. If capacity, parental responsibility or the applicable rules are unclear, refer the question to qualified legal, ethics or safeguarding reviewers.

## Build consent and assent into recruitment

Before contacting families, map everyone involved in the decision: the child, the person with parental responsibility, any school or community gatekeeper, the data controller and the safeguarding lead.

A teacher, recruiter or accompanying adult may arrange access. That does not automatically authorise them to give every permission required. Confirm each person’s role proportionately and collect only the verification data needed.

Give adults and children separate information designed for their needs. A parent should not be responsible for explaining the study to the child. Use short sentences, visual choices and an unambiguous stopping phrase, such as: “You can say stop, take a break, or choose not to answer.”

Watch for pressure from authority figures, group settings or incentives. Declining should not affect access to a service, and incentives should not depend on completing every task. Apply the same care when you [recruit participants for user research](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-to-recruit-participants-for-user-research/) and create a [participant incentive framework](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/user-research-participant-incentives-uk/).

Reconfirm assent when the session begins and before any materially different activity, such as recording a screen or discussing a sensitive subject.

## Choose age-appropriate UX research methods

Choose methods according to communication preferences, capacity, access needs and the product decision. Age can inform the plan, but it does not determine someone’s ability or willingness to participate.

| Design choice | Plan for |
|---|---|
| Task complexity | One clear action at a time; a demonstration where helpful |
| Session structure | Short blocks and planned breaks |
| Language | Concrete words and checks for understanding |
| Response mode | Talking, showing, sorting, drawing or pointing |
| Adult presence | A planned support role that does not answer for the child |
| Stop signals | Verbal and non-verbal ways to pause or finish |

Visual prompts, story-based scenarios, card sorting, drawing and demonstrations can make usability sessions more accessible. They do not replace assent. Make clear that you are assessing the product, not the child.

Allow processing time. Account for SEND, literacy, language, culture and group dynamics without inferring ability from age or diagnosis. A group session may reveal shared language while making disagreement harder to voice; use individual sessions where privacy or influence is a concern.

Before recruitment, pilot the child-facing explanation, activities and stopping mechanism with an appropriate reviewer or advisory participant. Then select the [UX research methods](/blog/ux-research-methods/) that fit the question rather than relying on rigid age bands.

## Create the safeguarding protocol before fieldwork

Write the safeguarding protocol before the first session. UKRI says appropriate child-protection or safeguarding measures should be in place at study outset ([UKRI](https://www.ukri.org/manage-your-award/good-research-resource-hub/research-with-children-and-young-people/)).

Name a safeguarding lead and deputy. Record contact routes for the host setting, your organisation and relevant local services. The statutory guidance linked here applies to England, so teams working elsewhere must confirm national and local requirements ([Department for Education](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/working-together-to-safeguard-children--2)).

Set out the moderator, observer, accompanying adult and chaperone roles before fieldwork. Avoid unplanned, unsupervised one-to-one contact, whether the session is in person or remote. Define conditions for pausing or stopping: refusal, distress, fatigue, unsafe content, loss of required adult presence or departure from the agreed protocol.

Prepare a response for disclosures. Listen calmly. Do not investigate, ask leading follow-up questions or promise secrecy. Record factual details securely, then escalate through the agreed route. Department for Education guidance advises against follow-up questions about potentially sensitive comments ([DfE](https://user-research.education.gov.uk/guidance/ethics-and-safeguarding/research-with-children-and-young-people)).

Agree emergency procedures, device controls, private-message boundaries, incident documentation and researcher debriefing. Whether someone needs a DBS check or equivalent depends on their role, activities, supervision, frequency and jurisdiction.

No agency case study is included here: the supplied evidence does not document one, and anonymising an unsupported account would not make it evidence.

## Minimise and manage children’s research data

Collect only the data the study needs. Children have data-protection rights, and organisations must help them understand and exercise those rights ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/children-and-the-uk-gdpr/what-data-protection-rights-do-children-have)).

Before fieldwork, document the data controller, purpose, lawful basis, processors or recipients, storage location, retention period and deletion process. Keep recordings, identifiers, contact details, school information and screen content to a minimum. Plan for accidental capture of family members, classmates or notifications.

Explain confidentiality limits before participation, especially where a safeguarding disclosure could require action. Where practical, store agreement records separately from research outputs. Give participants a workable way to ask questions, withdraw where applicable or exercise their rights. Explain what may happen to material already collected; do not promise deletion if the approved policy cannot provide it.

## Use the go/no-go worksheet: a filled example

This fictional worksheet is an illustration, not legal advice. It is a reusable pre-fieldwork decision record: replace the example evidence, owners and actions with the controls for your study.

| Control | Evidence | Owner | Status | Action required |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Necessity | Adults cannot reliably test child instructions | Research lead | Ready | None |
| Jurisdiction | England; school policy reviewed | Safeguarding lead | Ready | None |
| Participant profile | Ages, access needs and exclusions documented | Recruiter | Ready | None |
| Capacity approach | Child information checked for understanding | Moderator | Ready | None |
| Adult permission | Separate parent record and responsibility check | Recruiter | Ready | None |
| Child assent | Child-facing explanation and stop card | Moderator | Ready | None |
| Method adaptation | Short tasks, visual prompts and breaks | Research lead | Ready | None |
| Adult presence | Accompanying-adult role agreed | Host contact | Ready | None |
| Safeguarding owner | Named lead and deputy | Research lead | Ready | None |
| Disclosure route | Daytime route confirmed; out-of-hours route absent | Safeguarding lead | Revise | Confirm escalation contact |
| Data controls | Recording approved; retention period unclear | Privacy owner | Revise | Set and document retention |
| Stop conditions | Refusal, distress, fatigue and loss of adult presence | Moderator | Ready | None |

Outcome: **revise**. Fieldwork must wait until the out-of-hours escalation route and recording-retention period are confirmed.

Use the worksheet in this sequence: review the question, recruit, confirm permissions, brief the team, reconfirm assent, monitor willingness, close safely and document the outcome. Practical reminders can also [reduce participant no-shows](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/reduce-user-research-participant-no-shows/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do you need parental consent for UX research with children?

Usually, obtain appropriate adult permission alongside the child’s informed and continuing agreement. Review capacity, parental responsibility, setting, jurisdiction and study type rather than applying a universal rule.

### What is child assent in research?

Child assent is a child’s affirmative, informed and voluntary willingness to participate when another person may provide legal permission. Silence is not assent.

### Can a child withdraw from a UX research session?

Yes. Provide verbal and non-verbal ways to pause or stop, monitor willingness throughout the session and respect refusal.

### Does every UX researcher working with children need a DBS check?

There is no blanket rule. Check eligibility against the role, activities, supervision, frequency, jurisdiction, employer and host-setting requirements.

### What should a researcher do if a child makes a safeguarding disclosure?

Follow the agreed protocol: listen without investigating, avoid leading questions or promises of secrecy, record factual details securely and contact the designated safeguarding route promptly.
