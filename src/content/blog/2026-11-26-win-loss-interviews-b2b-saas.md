## What win-loss interviews reveal that CRM close reasons cannot

CRM close reasons are useful prompts, not a complete account of a B2B purchase. Win-loss interviews for B2B SaaS are post-decision conversations that reconstruct the buyer’s evaluation: the trigger, people involved, alternatives, criteria, turning point and outcome.

They differ from churn interviews, which examine an existing customer relationship; NPS, which captures a narrow sentiment signal; and seller debriefs, which record an internal view of the deal. Each can be useful. None replaces the buyer’s account of the decision.

Treat the CRM outcome as a hypothesis to test. A reason recorded as “price” may reflect a procurement constraint, migration risk, insufficient proof of value or a decision to keep an incumbent. One participant may also have limited visibility of the full decision. Research on key-informant accuracy recommends [triangulation of key-informant responses](https://www.martinreimann.com/pdf/Homburg%2C%20Klarmann%2C%20Reimann%2C%20Schilke.%20What%20Drives%20Key%20Informant%20Accuracy.pdf) where the decision warrants it.

The practical output is comparable evidence about the trigger, committee, criteria, alternatives, turning point and outcome. For programme-level context, see [win-loss analysis for B2B SaaS](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/win-loss-analysis-b2b-saas/).

## Choose a learning cohort before you recruit

Start each research round with one decision question. For example: why does a defined ideal customer profile choose an incumbent; what causes no-decision deals to stall; or which proof reduces implementation risk?

Build the cohort around that question. Include wins, losses and, where relevant, no-decisions. Segment before comparing responses: ICP, deal size, product line, buying motion and credible alternative can all alter the meaning of an answer. A loss to an incumbent in enterprise procurement is not directly comparable with a small-business no-decision.

Recruit people who experienced the evaluation. The evaluator or champion is often the first contact. Add an approver or technical reviewer when their role materially shaped the decision or when you need to compare accounts. Guidance on [B2B buying committee interviews](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/b2b-buying-committee-interviews/) can help define the sample.

Use an outreach tracker with:

- Close date and cohort
- Contact and decision role
- Account owner
- Invitation, booked and completed status
- Recording and consent status
- Known CRM outcome

Frame the invitation as research, not a delayed sales follow-up. Explain that the call will not reopen the deal. Check [participant interview integrity](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/detect-bots-deepfakes-research-participant-interviews/) before relying on participant input.

## Set up a neutral, consent-aware interview

Use an interviewer who did not own the deal. Sales can provide background beforehand, but should not attend. Protect any anonymity boundary promised to the participant.

Open in plain language: explain the research purpose, your non-sales role, whether recording is optional, how findings will be shared, anonymity limits, and how to withdraw or ask follow-up questions. Involve your privacy lead to determine the appropriate lawful basis in your jurisdiction. Where processing relies on consent, the ICO says consent should be specific and granular, with a record of what the person agreed to and when. See the [ICO’s consent guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/consent/how-should-we-obtain-record-and-manage-consent/).

Use a topic guide rather than a rigid script. This retains comparability while allowing the buyer’s sequence to lead the conversation. [Clozd recommends organising a discussion guide around topics, not a script](https://www.clozd.com/guides/win-loss-interviews).

A practical 30-minute flow is:

1. Decision context and status quo
2. Trigger and initial requirements
3. Shortlist and buying committee
4. Evaluation moments and evidence
5. Final decision and counterfactual
6. Reflection for peers

Use neutral probes: “What happened next?”, “Who needed to be comfortable with that?”, “What made that important?”, and “What would have needed to change?” Do not begin with “Was it price?” because the CRM says price. These principles also apply to broader [UX research methods](/blog/ux-research-methods/): provide enough structure to compare interviews without steering answers.

## Use win-loss interview questions that reconstruct the decision

Organise win-loss interview questions around the buyer journey, not internal departments. The aim is to reconstruct events and reasoning.

**Trigger and status quo**

- What prompted you to look for a solution?
- What was happening before you began evaluating options?
- What had changed if you did nothing?

**Requirements and shortlist**

- What did you need a solution to do?
- How did you hear about the options you considered?
- Which suppliers made the shortlist, and why?

**People and evaluation**

- Who was involved at each stage?
- What did each person need to be confident about?
- What evidence did you use to assess the options?

**Turning point and outcome**

- What moment most influenced the final direction?
- What made the chosen route preferable?
- What would have needed to change for a different outcome?

**Peer explanation**

- If a peer asked why you made this choice, what would you tell them?

Tailor probes to the participant. A technical evaluator may explain integration evidence and operational risk; an economic buyer may explain budget boundaries and expected value; a champion may explain internal momentum and objections.

When a buyer says “price”, ask which outcome, risk or constraint made the cost unacceptable. When they cite a feature, ask how its presence or absence affected the decision. Record whether a competitor or criterion was volunteered or introduced by your prompt. One interview is account-level evidence, not a prevalence claim.

For context on how buyers form shortlists before speaking to vendors, read [how B2B buyers use AI to research and shortlist vendors](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/how-b2b-buyers-use-ai-to-research-vendors/). The closest competing guide, [FieldSignal’s win-loss interviews playbook](https://fieldsignalhq.com/resources/blog/win-loss-interviews-for-b2b-saas-a-founder-s-playbook), also covers post-decision interviews, cohorts and question design.

## Fill the Deal-Decision Evidence Sheet after every call

Complete this copyable Deal-Decision Evidence Sheet immediately after each interview. It separates what the buyer said from what the CRM implies and creates a comparable record for synthesis.

### Deal-Decision Evidence Sheet

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Deal and cohort | Anonymous deal ID, outcome, segment and comparison group |
| Participant role | Champion, evaluator, approver or technical reviewer |
| Decision timeline | Trigger, shortlist, evaluation and decision sequence |
| Status quo | Existing process, supplier or no-action route |
| Alternatives | Options volunteered and options prompted |
| Criteria and evidence | Criteria, proof sought and evidence that mattered |
| Decision-maker map | Roles, influence and final authority |
| Turning point | Event or evidence that changed direction |
| Stated outcome | Buyer’s explanation of the decision |
| Counterfactual | What would have needed to change |
| Verbatim excerpt | Short anonymised quote supporting a finding |
| CRM record | Recorded close reason and notes |
| Discrepancy | Agreement, partial agreement or conflict |
| Confidence | High, medium or low, with reason |
| Owner and next step | Accountable team, action and review date |

**Illustrative hypothetical example:** CRM records “lost: too expensive”. The buyer describes a lower-cost incumbent, concern about migration effort and no convincing implementation plan for a technical reviewer. Record “price” as the CRM label, migration risk as supporting decision evidence, and the discrepancy as “price only”. Set confidence to medium because one participant was interviewed. Route the next step to product marketing and sales to review implementation proof, rather than assuming a price change is needed.

Use this routing matrix:

| Evidence pattern | Appropriate response |
|---|---|
| Repeated in a comparable cohort and high consequence | Assign an owner to test a change |
| Single account or contradictory accounts | Conduct further interviews |
| CRM and buyer account conflict | Review sales process, hand-offs or CRM fields |
| Clear unmet workflow or risk | Route to product discovery |

Do not infer a wider client finding from one account. Route evidence into action through [turn research into impact](/blog/insight-to-impact/).

## Turn patterns into positioning, product and sales actions

Synthesis should compare evidence by cohort and decision stage. Keep contradictions visible. A price pattern may mean a procurement ceiling in one segment and weak value proof in another; combining them too early produces an unhelpful conclusion.

Route findings to the team able to change the condition:

- **Positioning:** language, differentiation and proof gaps.
- **Sales enablement:** discovery questions, objection handling and collateral.
- **Product discovery:** unmet workflows, integration needs or perceived risk.
- **RevOps:** close-reason fields, qualification and hand-offs.

Create an action record with the evidence, hypothesis, owner, planned change, date, expected signal and review date. An expected signal might be better discovery notes or use of an implementation-proof asset. Do not promise a win-rate outcome from a small interview set.

Update the worksheet after every interview and review emerging evidence with cross-functional owners. Refresh the topic guide when priorities change. This is pre-purchase decision research, not a substitute for [churned customer interviews for B2B SaaS](https://blog.glasgow.works/blog/churned-customer-interviews-b2b-saas/), which examine why an existing relationship ended.

Choose one live decision question, recruit 5–8 comparable participants across relevant outcomes, complete the sheets, then review them before changing messaging or roadmap priorities. Where evidence points to an unmet workflow, take it into [product discovery](/blog/product-discovery/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Who should conduct win-loss interviews for B2B SaaS?

A researcher or internal partner who did not own the deal is usually best placed to conduct the interview. State the non-sales purpose clearly and honour any anonymity promise. Sales can provide context before the call but should not attend.

### What should you ask in a lost-deal interview?

Use open questions to reconstruct the trigger, shortlist, committee, evaluation, turning point, decision and counterfactual. Use neutral probes rather than starting from the CRM loss reason.

### Should you interview won deals as well as lost deals?

Yes. Wins show which messages and proof buyers retained, while losses expose barriers and alternatives. Compare both within similar cohorts rather than treating either group as representative alone.

### How do you turn interview findings into action?

Complete the Deal-Decision Evidence Sheet, then use the routing matrix to separate a repeated, high-consequence pattern from an isolated report. Assign an owner, a testable change and a review date.
