Research creates value when someone uses credible evidence to make a decision. A finding, a recommendation and a business outcome are different things. Keep the chain between them visible so a reader can assess what the study does and does not support.

## Build the evidence-to-decision chain

| Layer | Question it answers | Example, using a hypothetical setup study |
|---|---|---|
| Observation | What happened? | A participant stopped when asked for administrator access |
| Interpretation | What might explain it? | The person could not authorise the connection and did not know who could |
| Finding | What pattern is supported across evidence? | Setup depends on a handoff that the current flow does not explain |
| Recommendation | What should we investigate or change? | Try a clear request-to-administrator route |
| Decision | What will the team do? | Prototype the handoff before redesigning unrelated screens |
| Evaluation | Did the change help? | Observe handoff completion, failure and recovery in another round |

The example is a teaching scenario, not a measured client outcome. The distinction between observations and interpretation is also useful when [analysing a research session](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/analyse-a-research-session).

## Use a decision log people can challenge

Download the [decision log worksheet](/downloads/research-decision-log.csv), or copy these fields:

| Field | Filled example |
|---|---|
| Decision | Whether to add an administrator handoff |
| Owner | Activation product manager |
| Evidence | Session IDs and timestamps showing access barriers |
| Contrary evidence | Participants with administrator access completed setup |
| Confidence and limits | Directional qualitative finding; prevalence not measured |
| Action | Prototype an invitation with clear permission information |
| Follow-up | Observe authorised and unauthorised users attempting the flow |
| Review trigger | After the next study round; sooner if security requirements change |

Do not fill missing evidence with a stronger adjective. If the claim rests on one session, record it as a question to investigate. If independent sources disagree, preserve the disagreement rather than averaging it into a false consensus.

## Choose the right analysis

Use [thematic analysis](/blog/thematic-analysis-qualitative-research/) for an interpretive account of patterns in qualitative data. Use an [evidence matrix](/blog/how-to-analyze-user-interview-data/) when the team needs to inspect extracts across participants or cases. Neither makes a small purposive sample representative of your market.

When findings compete for attention, [prioritise opportunities](/blog/opportunity-prioritisation-product-research/) by the decision and cost of being wrong. A severe failure affecting a small, important group can deserve attention even if it is not the most frequently mentioned theme.

## Make the readout useful

Lead with the decision, the strongest relevant evidence and its limitations. Give stakeholders a short written record they can revisit, alongside access-controlled supporting material. [GOV.UK's guidance on sharing findings](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/sharing-user-research-findings) describes ways to make research available to a team.

Choose a [research report format](/blog/how-to-write-a-ux-research-report/) and [stakeholder readout](/blog/communicating-research-findings-to-stakeholders/) appropriate to the decision. A long deck is not evidence of impact.

## Measure the result honestly

Record whether research changed a decision, then separately measure what happened after the change. Adoption, retention and revenue can move for many reasons. Without a suitable comparison, report an observed change rather than attributing it entirely to research.

Use [research ROI and influence measures](/blog/how-to-measure-ux-research-roi/) to select the appropriate claim. Keep unresolved questions in the [research debt register](/blog/research-debt-in-product-teams/) so they remain visible after the readout.
